## 1. Definition

**The Reserve Currency Cycle** is the historical pattern by which a currency rises to, holds, and eventually loses its status as the world's (or a region's) dominant reserve currency — a status distinct from ordinary currency devaluation, driven by a country's relative economic/political decline combined with chronic monetization of its debt.

## 2. Intuition

- Mechanism (three-part pattern, per the source, derived from the Dutch guilder, British pound, and US dollar cases): (1) an already-established, ongoing loss of economic and political primacy relative to a rising rival power creates the underlying vulnerability; (2) large and growing government debts are increasingly monetized — the central bank prints money to buy its own government's debt rather than letting real interest rates rise to attract genuine buyers; (3) this combination produces a weakening currency in a self-reinforcing "run" (holders sell the currency and the debt denominated in it) that becomes unstoppable once the fiscal and balance-of-payments deficits are too large for spending cutbacks alone to close.
- Devaluation vs. reserve-status loss — these are related but genuinely different events, not synonyms: a currency can devalue substantially and *keep* its reserve-currency status if the issuing country's relative economic and military power remains dominant (the source's example: the US devalued against gold in 1933 and 1971, and more gradually since 2000, without losing reserve status). Losing reserve-currency status specifically requires *chronic and large* devaluation *combined with* a genuine, sustained decline in relative power — devaluation alone is not sufficient, and the condition that determines which outcome occurs is whether the underlying relative-power decline is also present.
- Speed varies by case, and the source ties the variation to how much relative power the country retains at the moment of devaluation: the Dutch guilder's loss of reserve status was fast (well under a decade), coinciding directly with the Netherlands losing a major war and facing invasion — an abrupt, war-driven loss of power. The British pound's decline was gradual, taking two separate devaluation episodes (1949 and 1967) over roughly two decades, because the UK retained more residual economic and political standing even after "losing" primacy to the US.
- Leading indicators the source identifies as empirically preceding the devaluation event itself (useful because they are observable before the crisis is obvious): falling net central-bank reserves, sometimes starting years ahead of the devaluation, and the suspension of currency convertibility ahead of a formal devaluation (e.g. the UK suspended pound convertibility in 1947, about two years before the 1949 devaluation; the US ended dollar convertibility in 1971).
- What is required for a *rising* currency to actually assume reserve-currency status (not just for an incumbent to lose it): the source argues markets require the currency to be freely convertible, backed by durable trust in the rule of law and property-rights protection, and supported by deep, open capital markets — a rising economic/military power's currency does not automatically inherit reserve status just because the country's overall power is rising if these structural/trust conditions aren't also met (the source's example: the Chinese renminbi's capital controls and less-established rule-of-law protections for foreign capital, at time of writing).

## 4. When it matters

- Long-horizon currency-risk and reserve-allocation decisions: distinguishing "this currency is devaluing" from "this currency is at risk of losing reserve status" changes the appropriate response — the former is a recurring, survivable event even for dominant reserve currencies; the latter is a much rarer, structurally different regime shift.
- Monitoring leading indicators (central-bank reserve trends, convertibility restrictions/suspensions) as an early-warning framework for currency stress, ahead of the devaluation event itself becoming visible in the exchange rate.
- Assessing whether a rising economic power's currency (e.g. renminbi, historically the deutsche mark or yen) is a credible reserve-currency contender: convertibility, rule-of-law trust, and open deep capital markets are necessary conditions the source argues are separate from, and do not automatically follow from, a country's rising economic/military power.

## 6. Related Concepts

- [[C - Long-Term Debt Cycle]] — the Reserve Currency Cycle is the specific, rarer outcome that occurs when chronic devaluation (the Long-Term Debt Cycle's crisis/devaluation stage) combines with a genuine, sustained loss of the issuing country's relative power; every reserve-currency loss the source studies is driven by an underlying debt cycle, but not every debt-cycle devaluation causes a reserve-currency loss.

## 7. Pitfalls

- The pattern is derived from a small number of historical cases (Dutch guilder, British pound, US dollar) — the source itself frames this as case-based pattern recognition, not a statistically estimated model with a known error rate; applying it to predict a specific future transition (e.g. dollar to renminbi) requires treating it as a qualitative checklist of conditions, not a quantitative forecast.
- Devaluation and reserve-status loss are easy to conflate in market commentary; treating any dollar devaluation as evidence of imminent reserve-status loss would misapply this Concept — the source explicitly notes the US devalued twice (1933, 1971) without losing reserve status.

## 8. Minimal Example

- The British pound case: the UK won both World Wars yet still lost reserve-currency status gradually, because its debts grew much larger than its assets and its competitiveness declined relative to the rising US — illustrating that winning a war does not prevent reserve-currency loss if the underlying debt and relative-competitiveness trends are unfavorable, and confirming that the mechanism is about relative economic/financial trajectory, not military victory alone.
