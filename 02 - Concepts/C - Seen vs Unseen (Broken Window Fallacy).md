## 1. Definition

**Seen vs. Unseen reasoning (the Broken Window Fallacy)** is the principle that a sound assessment of any economic act or policy must account for its unseen, indirect, delayed, or diffused effects — not only its seen, immediate, concentrated effects on one visible group. The fallacy named after Bastiat's broken-window parable is crediting a destructive or redistributive act with creating net value because its visible, immediate effect (e.g. new business for a glazier) is mistaken for the whole picture, while the unseen forgone alternative (e.g. the tailor's lost business) is ignored.

## 2. Intuition

- Mechanism: any transaction or policy that is visible and immediate (new spending, a new job, a rebuilt structure) draws attention naturally; the alternative use of the same resources that the transaction crowds out is, by construction, never observed — it simply never happens, so there is nothing to see. This asymmetry in *visibility*, not in actual economic weight, is what drives the fallacy: people systematically overweight what is shown and underweight what is displaced.
- The two sides are not automatically equal or opposite in magnitude — the discipline the concept demands is tracing *both* sides explicitly (what was gained, and what alternative was forgone to get it) rather than assuming either that visible activity is proof of net gain, or reflexively that all visible activity nets to zero. Genuine net gains and net losses both exist; the fallacy is skipping the unseen side of the ledger entirely.
- Applied to markets and policy analysis: any intervention that visibly benefits one group (a subsidy, a tariff, a price control, a stimulus) should be evaluated against its unseen cost to the group(s) whose resources were diverted or whose price signal was distorted to produce that visible benefit — see [[C - Price System as Information Mechanism]] for the allocation mechanism this reasoning protects.

## 3. Mathematical perspective (if applicable)

No single formula; the analytical discipline is $\text{Net effect} = \text{Seen effect} - \text{Unseen (forgone) effect}$, where the unseen term is unobserved directly and must be inferred from counterfactual reasoning (what would the same resources otherwise have funded/produced).

## 4. When it matters

- Evaluating any policy, market intervention, or narrative that claims a destructive or redistributive event is "good for the economy" purely by pointing to visible resulting activity (post-disaster reconstruction spending, a subsidized industry's job count, a tariff-protected sector's employment).
- A general discipline for market/policy analysis: distinguishing a genuine net creation of value from a mere transfer or diversion of existing resources that happens to be visible.

## 6. Related Concepts

- [[C - Opportunity Cost]] — the unseen side of the ledger this concept insists on tracing is, precisely, the opportunity cost of the resources consumed by the seen/visible act.
- [[C - Price System as Information Mechanism]] — interventions that create a seen benefit by overriding a market price typically do so by suppressing the very signal that would otherwise reveal the unseen cost.

## 7. Pitfalls

- Overcorrecting into "all government/interventionist spending nets to zero" as a blanket rule — the concept requires tracing the actual unseen alternative in each case, not assuming a fixed offset.
- Confusing this with a claim that visible economic activity never represents real value — much visible activity is genuine net production; the fallacy specifically concerns activity that exists only because it displaced an equal or greater unseen alternative (e.g. destruction-driven "demand" rather than genuine net capital formation).

## 8. Minimal Example

- A vandal breaks a baker's shop window. The baker pays a glazier $50 to replace it — visible new business for the glazier. But the baker was planning to spend that $50 on a new suit; the tailor's business that would have resulted is the unseen loss. Net result: the community has a window (which it already had) and has lost a suit it would otherwise have gained — it is poorer by one suit, not richer by $50 of glazier activity. Source: [[Economics in One Lesson]], Chapter 2.
