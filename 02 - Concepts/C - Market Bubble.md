## 1. Definition

**Market Bubble** is an episode in which the price of an asset or asset class is driven, by collective self-reinforcing speculative buying, substantially above any plausible estimate of its fundamental/intrinsic value, followed by a sharp collapse once the speculative buying can no longer be sustained.

## 2. Intuition

- Mechanism: a positive feedback loop. Rising prices attract attention and new buyers (via [[C - Herd Behavior]] and [[C - Castle in the Air Theory]] belief-anticipation); new buying pushes prices higher still; higher prices and visible profits attract yet more buyers. The loop terminates when the pool of prospective new buyers is exhausted — the "greater fool" runs out — at which point even a modest wave of selling can trigger a collapse of similar or greater speed than the run-up.
- What determines whether a given episode of rising prices is a genuine bubble versus a fundamentals-justified rally is, in real time, extremely hard to determine — Malkiel explicitly notes (Ch. 11) that the same valuation models that correctly flagged the 1999-2000 dot-com bubble also incorrectly flagged the market as "overpriced" in 1992 and 1996, years before subsequent strong fundamentals-justified returns. This is presented as a genuine epistemic limitation, not resolved by the book: bubbles are far easier to identify in hindsight than in real time.
- Bubbles are often accompanied by, but not solely caused by, outright fraud (e.g. ZZZZ Best, Enron) — fraud is a symptom/enabler that flourishes in the same overoptimistic conditions, not the fundamental cause of the mispricing.

## 4. When it matters

- Recognizing the structural signature of a bubble (invented valuation metrics used to justify prices conventional metrics cannot support, widespread media/social enthusiasm, rapidly rising new-issue/IPO activity) is a practical risk-management skill for any investor or trader, even though the book stresses that timing an exit from a bubble in real time is not reliably possible.

## 6. Related Concepts

## 7. Pitfalls

- Believing a bubble can be reliably identified and timed in real time from valuation metrics alone — the book's own example (Greenspan's December 1996 "irrational exuberance" speech, followed by ~7.5%/year returns through 2013) shows an early, correct-in-spirit warning can still be years too early to act on profitably.
- Treating every period of rapid, narrative-driven price appreciation as necessarily a bubble; some such episodes are subsequently justified by fundamentals.

## 8. Minimal Example

- The Internet/dot-com bubble (1998-2000): new, non-standard valuation metrics were invented to justify unprofitable companies' prices once P/E multiples could no longer do so; the NASDAQ subsequently fell roughly 80% from its peak. Source: [[A Random Walk Down Wall Street]], Chapters 4, 11.
