## 1. Definition

**Anchoring** is the systematic bias by which an initial number presented to a person — even one known to be arbitrary or irrelevant — measurably biases their subsequent numerical estimate or judgment toward it.

## 2. Intuition

- Mechanism: anchoring operates through two distinct pathways. (1) **Insufficient adjustment** — when a person considers the anchor as a candidate answer and deliberately adjusts away from it (an effortful, System-2-mediated process), they consistently stop adjusting too soon, leaving the final estimate biased toward the anchor. (2) **Priming / selective accessibility** — merely being exposed to a number activates anchor-compatible evidence and associations in memory (an automatic, System-1 process), biasing judgment even when no deliberate "adjustment" process occurs at all, and even under time pressure or cognitive load that should suppress System-2 involvement.
- What determines the size of the effect: the anchor need not be plausible or relevant — a documented experiment spun a wheel-of-fortune rigged to land on either 10 or 65, then asked subjects an unrelated question (the percentage of African countries in the UN); subjects who saw 65 gave substantially higher estimates than subjects who saw 10, despite knowing the wheel's outcome was random and had no bearing on the question.
- Because anchoring operates partly through an automatic priming pathway, awareness of the bias and explicit instruction to ignore the anchor reduce but do not reliably eliminate its effect — similar to other System-1-driven biases documented in [[F - System 1 and System 2 (Dual-Process Thinking)]].

## 4. When it matters

- Negotiation and price discovery: the first number stated in a negotiation (a first offer, a broker's suggested price, an analyst's initial price target) exerts a measurable pull on the final agreed price or subsequent estimates, independent of its actual justification.
- Valuation and forecasting: an analyst's or trader's estimate can be anchored to an irrelevant recent reference point (e.g. a stock's own recent high/low, a round number, a previous forecast) rather than to fresh, independently-weighted evidence — a specific mechanism by which stale information can continue to distort current judgment.
- Designing decision processes: because the priming pathway operates automatically, procedural safeguards (e.g. requiring an independent estimate before any anchor is disclosed, "premortem"-style structured elicitation) are more reliable than simply instructing a decision-maker to "ignore" a known anchor.

## 6. Related Concepts

- [[C - Overconfidence Bias]] — mentioned there in passing (forecaster herding "close to a simple extrapolation of the recent trend") as a related but distinct mechanism; anchoring is the more general cognitive bias underlying that specific herding pattern.

## 7. Pitfalls

- Anchoring is not limited to numerical estimation under genuine uncertainty — it affects judgments even when the anchor is explicitly known to be random or irrelevant, which distinguishes it from a rational, evidence-weighing use of a relevant reference point.

## 8. Minimal Example

- Real-estate agents and students given an identical house to appraise, with only the listed asking price varied, produced substantially different valuations correlated with the listed price — including agents who denied that the listed price had influenced their judgment at all.
