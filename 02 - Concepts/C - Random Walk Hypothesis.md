## 1. Definition

**Random Walk Hypothesis** is the claim that successive stock-price changes are statistically independent of past price changes, so that a security's future short-run price movements cannot be predicted from its past price history. It is the foundational premise behind the weak form of the [[C - Efficient Market Hypothesis]].

## 2. Intuition

- If price changes were serially correlated (momentum or mean-reversion strong enough to survive costs), technicians could exploit the pattern by trading on it — but that very trading would tend to eliminate the exploitable pattern, pushing prices back toward randomness. The random walk is therefore not an assumption that markets are chaotic, but a consequence of markets reacting quickly to new (inherently unpredictable) information: prices move randomly precisely *because* the market is efficient, not despite it.
- A useful check on intuition: sequences generated by fair coin flips (heads = price up, tails = price down) produce charts that are visually indistinguishable from real stock-price charts, including apparent "head-and-shoulders" and cyclical patterns — demonstrating that pattern recognition on price charts is highly vulnerable to statistical illusion (apophenia). This does not mean prices are *literally* a coin flip; it means the degree of serial dependence, if any, is too weak and unstable to be reliably exploited after transaction costs and taxes.
- Competing view: some later research (momentum effects, short-horizon return continuation — see the "smart beta" momentum strategy discussed under [[C - Smart Beta]]) finds statistically detectable departures from a pure random walk. The book's own position (Ch. 11) is that such patterns exist but are typically too weak, inconsistent across periods, or too costly to trade to constitute a genuine exploitable edge — a "weak-form-mostly-true, not-perfectly-true" reading rather than a strict denial of any predictability.

## 3. Mathematical perspective (if applicable)

$$P_t = P_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \quad E[\varepsilon_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = 0$$

Price at time $t$ equals the prior price plus a mean-zero, unpredictable innovation given all information $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ available at $t-1$.

## 4. When it matters

- Justifies skepticism toward chart-pattern-based technical trading rules (filter systems, Dow theory, relative strength, "hemline"/"Super Bowl" folk indicators).
- Underlies the case for passive/index investing: if short-run prices are unpredictable, market timing and stock-picking based on past-price information cannot be relied on to add value net of costs.

## 6. Related Concepts

- [[C - Efficient Market Hypothesis]] — the random walk is essentially the weak form of EMH applied to price history specifically.
- [[C - Market Bubble]] — bubbles are episodes where the random-walk/EMH premise appears to fail most visibly, at least in hindsight.

## 7. Pitfalls

- Random-walk behavior in prices does not imply that price *levels* are "correct" or that markets are perfectly efficient — only that the *next* change is not predictable from the *past* sequence of changes. Confusing "unpredictable changes" with "correct prices" is a common misreading.
- Apparent chart patterns are not reliable evidence against the random walk; the human visual system reliably finds patterns in genuinely random sequences (see the coin-toss chart experiment in the Minimal Example below).

## 8. Minimal Example

- Malkiel's classroom experiment: students construct a hypothetical stock chart starting at $50, moving up or down $0.50 on each successive coin flip (heads = up, tails = down). The resulting charts are visually indistinguishable from real historical stock-price charts and even display apparent chartist patterns (inverted head-and-shoulders, breakouts) that a professional technician judged tradeable — despite being generated by a purely random process with no serial dependence. Source: [[A Random Walk Down Wall Street]], Chapter 6.
