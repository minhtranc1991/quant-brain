## 1. Definition

**Herd Behavior** is the tendency of individual investors' decisions to become correlated with, rather than independent of, other investors' decisions — driven by social proof, word-of-mouth contagion, and conformity pressure — such that errors of judgment reinforce rather than cancel out across the market.

## 2. Intuition

- Ordinary "wisdom of crowds" reasoning (many independent forecasts averaging out errors, à la the free-market price system) depends critically on individual decisions being *independent*. Herding breaks that independence: once investors start reacting to *each other's* behavior rather than to independent private information/analysis, the crowd stops averaging out error and starts amplifying it — a small initial imbalance can snowball into a large, self-reinforcing move.
- Experimental grounding: in Solomon Asch's conformity experiments, a lone subject presented with a visually obvious correct answer would frequently give a knowingly wrong answer simply because six confederates had already stated it — and a 2005 fMRI study (Berns) found that going along with the group activated brain regions associated with visual/spatial perception, not just conflict-monitoring, suggesting social pressure can genuinely alter perception, not merely override it.
- What determines the *strength* of herding in a given market episode: word-of-mouth density (Hong/Kubik/Stein find "social" households — those who interact with neighbors or attend church — invest in the market more, controlling for wealth/education/risk tolerance) and geographic/professional proximity (mutual-fund managers were found more likely to hold similar stocks when other managers in the same city held similar portfolios) both amplify contagion beyond what independent analysis would produce.

## 4. When it matters

- Explains the amplification mechanism inside [[C - Market Bubble]] episodes.
- Practical implication (Chapter 10's first behavioral-finance lesson): any investment that becomes a widespread topic of conversation among friends/media is flagged as a warning sign, not a buy signal, precisely because herding — not independent analysis — is likely driving the enthusiasm.
- Explains mutual-fund flow timing patterns: net inflows to equity funds peaked at the 2000 market top and cratered at the 2002 and 2008-09 troughs, a pattern consistent with herding rather than independent, contrarian analysis.

## 7. Pitfalls

- Herding is not limited to unsophisticated retail investors — the book documents institutional mutual-fund managers and even hedge funds herding into the same trades (e.g. hedge funds were net *buyers* of Internet stocks throughout 1998-early 2000, riding rather than correcting the bubble).
- The "wisdom of crowds" argument (that aggregate market prices are usually more accurate than any individual forecast) and herd behavior are not contradictory — the book presents both: crowds are usually well-calibrated when individual decisions are reasonably independent, and specifically pathological when herding dominates.

## 8. Minimal Example

- Robert Shiller and John Pound's survey of 131 individual investors found a typical response for what drew attention to their most recently purchased stock was a personal recommendation from a friend or relative — direct evidence of word-of-mouth-driven, non-independent decision-making. Source: [[A Random Walk Down Wall Street]], Chapter 10.
