# Chapter 17 — Regression to the Mean

## Summary

Kahneman's own account of discovering regression to the mean while teaching Israeli Air Force flight instructors: instructors believed praise after a good landing caused subsequent worse performance and criticism after a poor landing caused subsequent improvement, when both patterns are the statistically expected regression-to-the-mean pattern for any noisy, imperfectly-repeatable skill measure, independent of any real effect of praise or criticism.

Text: Flight instructors observing that cadets praised for an unusually good landing tended to perform worse next time, while cadets criticized after an unusually poor landing tended to perform better next time, concluded that punishment works and reward backfires — but both patterns are the expected statistical signature of regression to the mean for any noisy, imperfectly repeatable skill (an extreme landing reflects both underlying skill and a chance component that does not repeat), independent of any causal effect of the instructor's feedback; this produced a durable, false causal belief precisely because 'regression is not a causally interesting fact' and the causal-story-generating mind fills the vacuum with the misattributed feedback-consequence story instead.
