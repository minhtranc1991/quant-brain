## Summary

The 'Linda problem': subjects judge 'Linda is a bank teller and is active in the feminist movement' as more probable than 'Linda is a bank teller' alone — a logical impossibility (the conjunction fallacy), because the added detail makes the sketch more representative of the stereotype, overriding the elementary logical constraint that a conjunction cannot exceed either of its conjuncts in probability.

Text: Representativeness judgments routinely override both base-rate information (the Tom W experiment: subjects rank a candidate's likely field of study by stereotype-fit, largely ignoring the true relative enrollment base rates across fields) and elementary probability logic itself (the Linda 'conjunction fallacy': a conjunction of two stereotype-fitting details is judged more probable than either detail alone, even though a conjunction cannot logically exceed either of its constituent probabilities) — evidence that representativeness/similarity judgments and true probabilistic (Bayesian, base-rate-respecting) reasoning are computed by different, competing processes, with representativeness frequently winning even among statistically trained subjects.
