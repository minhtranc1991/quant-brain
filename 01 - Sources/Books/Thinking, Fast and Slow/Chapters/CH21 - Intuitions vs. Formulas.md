## Summary

Presents Paul Meehl's finding (and subsequent ~200-study literature) that simple statistical/actuarial formulas match or outperform expert clinical judgment across a wide range of prediction tasks (parole violation, treatment outcome, business failure, corporate bond risk), because formulas apply cues consistently while human judgment introduces uncontrolled noise from mood, fatigue, and case order, even when the human has access to strictly more information than the formula.

Text: Across a wide range of prediction domains studied since Paul Meehl's original 1954 review (parole violation, psychiatric/medical prognosis, business failure, corporate bond default risk, among others), simple statistical or actuarial formulas built from a small number of consistently-applied predictor variables match or outperform expert human judgment, including judgment by experts with access to strictly more case information than the formula uses — the mechanism is not that experts lack relevant knowledge, but that human judgment introduces uncontrolled inconsistency (the same expert reviewing the identical case on different days, in different moods, or in a different order of presentation can reach different conclusions) that a fixed formula, by construction, cannot.
