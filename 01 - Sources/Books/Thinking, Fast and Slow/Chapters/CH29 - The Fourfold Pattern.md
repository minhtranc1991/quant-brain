# Chapter 29 — The Fourfold Pattern

## Summary

Derives the 'fourfold pattern' of risk attitudes from prospect theory's probability weighting function (which overweights small probabilities and underweights moderate-to-high probabilities): risk-averse for high-probability gains and low-probability losses (paying a premium to lock in a likely gain, or to insure against an unlikely large loss), but risk-seeking for low-probability gains and high-probability losses (buying lottery tickets; litigating rather than accepting an unfavorable but likely settlement).

Text: Prospect theory's probability weighting function systematically overweights small probabilities and underweights moderate-to-high probabilities relative to their true values, producing a 'fourfold pattern' of risk attitudes that pure loss aversion alone cannot explain: risk-averse for high-probability gains (preferring a certain smaller gain to a risky larger one) and for low-probability losses (buying insurance/lottery-adjacent protection against a large but unlikely loss, paying more than its expected cost), but risk-seeking for low-probability gains (buying lottery tickets despite negative expected value) and for high-probability losses (rejecting a favorable settlement to gamble on a likely-but-not-certain worse trial outcome) — this pattern is a distinguishing empirical signature of prospect theory versus standard expected-utility theory, which predicts uniform risk aversion.
