## Summary

Explains why probability weighting distorts rare-event pricing in two opposite directions depending on vividness: vivid, easily-imagined, emotionally salient rare events (terrorism, plane crashes, jackpot wins) are overweighted and overpaid-for/overinsured-against, while abstract, non-vivid, statistically-described rare events that have not personally been experienced tend to be neglected or rounded down to zero ('this hasn't happened, so it won't') — the same probability-weighting machinery produces overpricing or underpricing of a rare event depending on how it is cognitively represented, not on its true probability alone.

Text: Whether a rare event is overpriced or underpriced by market participants depends on how vividly and concretely it is cognitively represented, not on its true probability alone: a vivid, emotionally salient, easily-imagined rare event (a specific plane crash, a specific terrorist attack, a lottery jackpot) tends to be overweighted relative to its true probability (consistent with prospect theory's probability-weighting function, Ch29) and can be over-insured-against or over-invested-in; an abstract, statistically-described rare event that has not been personally or vividly experienced tends instead to be neglected, rounded down toward zero, or not consciously considered as a live possibility at all — meaning the same underlying probability-weighting mechanism that explains lottery-ticket-buying and over-insurance can also explain systematic underpricing of unfamiliar tail risk, depending on which side of the vividness divide the event falls on.
