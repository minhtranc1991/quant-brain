## 1. Definition

**Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT)** is a mathematical model that formalizes [[C - Diversification]]'s implication for expected return using *multiple* systematic risk factors — developed by Stephen Ross — generalizing [[M - Capital Asset Pricing Model]]'s single beta factor to several macroeconomic sensitivities (e.g. to national income, interest rates, and inflation) on the premise that a single market-index beta is too crude a proxy to capture all the ways a security's returns co-move systematically with the broader economy.

## 2. Intuition

- Shares CAPM's core insight — only systematic (non-diversifiable) risk should be compensated — but questions whether a single stock-index beta fully captures "systematic." Any economic variable that affects many companies' returns simultaneously (and therefore cannot be diversified away by holding many stocks) is a candidate systematic risk factor in its own right, even if it is not fully captured by co-movement with a broad stock index.
- Concrete factors the source discusses as economically systematic and not fully captured by ordinary stock-index beta: changes in national income (mirrors changes in individuals' labor income — e.g. an autoworker's job and Ford stock both suffer in the same downturns, a systematic risk specific to that exposure), changes in interest rates (affect equity valuations broadly and are especially relevant for institutional portfolios also holding fixed-income assets), and changes in the inflation rate (raises rates, squeezes margins for firms whose costs outpace pricing power like utilities, while benefiting natural-resource firms) — each is argued to have a systematic (non-diversifiable), but distinct-from-beta, effect on returns.
- What determines whether APT improves on CAPM in practice: the source reports that statistical tests adding these systematic risk variables to beta produce "somewhat promising" better explanations of the cross-section of returns than beta alone — a qualified, not decisive, empirical endorsement — while also noting APT's risk-factor estimates are subject to some of the same measurement difficulties (proxy sensitivity, instability) that afflict beta itself.

## 3. Mathematical perspective

$$E(R_i) = R_f + \beta_{i,1}\,F_1 + \beta_{i,2}\,F_2 + \dots + \beta_{i,k}\,F_k$$

Where:
- $E(R_i)$ — expected return of security $i$
- $R_f$ — risk-free rate
- $\beta_{i,k}$ — security $i$'s sensitivity ("factor loading") to systematic risk factor $k$ (e.g. national income, interest rates, inflation)
- $F_k$ — the risk premium associated with factor $k$

_(The book presents APT narratively/conceptually and does not derive this general multi-factor equation explicitly; it is the standard formalization of the theory's stated logic, generalizing the single-factor CAPM equation.)_

## 4. Assumptions

- Returns are generated by a linear combination of a small number of systematic (macro) factors plus firm-specific noise that diversification eliminates — the same diversification-eliminates-unsystematic-risk logic as CAPM, extended to multiple factors.
- The chosen macro variables (national income, interest rates, inflation, in the book's discussion) are the economically relevant systematic factors, and are measurable/estimable with reasonable precision — an assumption the book flags is not fully clean in practice.

## 5. Estimation / Training Procedure

- Factor loadings (sensitivities) for each security are estimated via regression of historical returns against the chosen macro factor series, analogous to how CAPM beta is estimated against a market index, but across multiple factor series simultaneously.

## 6. When it matters in Finance

- A more complete framework than CAPM alone for assessing systematic risk exposure of portfolios that are especially sensitive to specific macro variables — e.g. bond-heavy institutional portfolios (interest-rate sensitivity) or portfolios concentrated in interest-rate-sensitive sectors like utilities.

## 8. Related Models

- [[M - Capital Asset Pricing Model]] — APT directly generalizes CAPM's single-beta framework to multiple systematic factors; both share the underlying premise that only non-diversifiable risk is priced.
- [[M - Fama-French Three-Factor Model]] — a related but distinct multi-factor extension that uses empirically-derived firm characteristics (size, value) rather than macroeconomic variables as its additional factors.

## 10. Limitations / Pitfalls

- Improved statistical fit over CAPM in the book's cited tests is described as only "somewhat promising," not decisive.
- Like CAPM's beta, APT's macro factor sensitivities are subject to measurement/estimation instability and are not free of the same proxy-selection problems that afflict single-factor beta.

## 11. Minimal Example

- The utility industry is presented as a case where a single beta measure fails to capture real systematic risk: utility earnings in the 1970s and early 2000s were driven substantially off-course by unpredictable regulatory rulings and sharp swings in the international price of oil (an interest-rate/inflation-adjacent systematic factor) — risks not well captured by the sector's stock-market beta alone. Source: [[A Random Walk Down Wall Street]], Chapter 9.
